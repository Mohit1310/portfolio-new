# Portfolio Context

This document is the north star for the portfolio. It records the direction that feels authentic and should be consulted before changing the visual design, content, or structure.

## Core direction

The portfolio should feel like an **explorer's workshop**: a place where a curious developer documents useful things, side quests, experiments, and the thinking behind the work.

The site should feel personal and considered, but not over-produced. It is a record of a person who follows interesting questions from the interface all the way into the system underneath it.

## Personal story

The owner:

- Started as a frontend developer.
- Grew into full-stack development by following curiosity beyond the interface.
- Finds software broadly interesting and enjoys understanding how things work.
- Has worked with Blender and should be able to show visual/3D experiments as part of the same curiosity.
- Tends to procrastinate and overthink. This can be acknowledged with warmth and self-awareness, but should be presented as a work habit being managed—not as the entire personality or a gimmick.

The central idea is:

> A curious, product-minded builder with a frontend eye, full-stack hands, and a habit of following interesting problems further than expected.

## Visual identity to preserve

The existing visual language is intentionally approved:

- Keep the distinctive typography and font pairing.
- Keep the solid, playful accent colors.
- Keep the contrast between a strong base color and bright accents rather than drifting toward a quiet corporate palette.
- Keep the sense of energy, warmth, and visual confidence.
- Keep playful details restrained enough that the work remains easy to understand.

The design should feel designed by a person with taste—not like a default developer template and not like a corporate resume.

Useful visual metaphors include field notes, a workbench, maps, coordinates, annotations, diagrams, sketches, labels, and collected artifacts. These should support the content, not become decoration for its own sake.

## Voice and writing

The voice is:

- Curious, direct, warm, and lightly self-aware.
- Confident about the work without sounding like marketing copy.
- Specific about decisions, trade-offs, and lessons.
- Comfortable admitting that some things are still in progress.

Avoid:

- Empty phrases such as “passionate developer,” “pixel perfect,” or “I build scalable solutions.”
- Listing technologies as the main personality.
- Overselling unfinished experiments.
- Making procrastination or overthinking sound like a cute brand identity.
- Writing a long biography before showing anything made.

## Recommended content shape

The site should communicate the story in this order:

1. **Point of view** — a short, memorable opening that says what kind of builder this is.
2. **Selected work** — a small number of strong projects with context and evidence.
3. **The lab / side quests** — Blender work, prototypes, visual studies, technical experiments, and unfinished-but-interesting explorations.
4. **About** — the journey from frontend to full stack, written as a story rather than a list of roles.
5. **Contact** — a friendly invitation to bring an interesting problem.

## Project storytelling

Each project should answer more than “what stack did you use?” Include, where possible:

- What was the problem or question?
- What did you personally own?
- What was difficult or uncertain?
- Which decision changed the outcome?
- What shipped or what was learned?

A smaller project with clear judgment is more valuable than a large project described only with features and tools.

## How to use personal details

Personal details should make the work memorable and human:

- Use Blender as evidence of visual curiosity and spatial thinking.
- Use the frontend-to-full-stack journey as evidence of depth and range.
- Use overthinking as a bridge to discussing trade-offs, iteration, and learning to ship.
- Use small notes, sketches, experiments, and observations to show how the person thinks between projects.

The portfolio should not pretend to be perfectly linear. Curiosity and changing direction are part of the story.

## Design guardrails for future changes

Before adding a section, animation, illustration, or effect, ask:

1. Does this reveal something true about the person or the work?
2. Does it strengthen the explorer/workshop feeling?
3. Does it preserve the approved typography, color energy, and playfulness?
4. Does it help a visitor understand or remember something?

If the answer is no, leave it out.

Do not replace the current personality with:

- A generic dark developer dashboard.
- A muted editorial redesign that removes the playful color.
- A technology logo wall.
- A resume-first layout.
- Random 3D decoration with no connection to the story.

## Future content to supply

The visual direction is established, but the portfolio will become truly personal when these placeholders are replaced:

- Real name and preferred short introduction.
- Three to five genuine project stories.
- Links, screenshots, or recordings of shipped work.
- Blender renders or experiments worth showing.
- A few short notes or lessons from actual work.
- Contact email and relevant social/profile links.

The most important next step is not adding more sections. It is replacing generic copy with specific evidence and real artifacts.
